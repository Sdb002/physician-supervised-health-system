## Which surveys or systematic reviews define health-aware food recommendation?

### Takeaway
The field is defined by a handful of food-recommender surveys (Trattner & Elsweiler 2017 preprint; Tran et al. 2018; Min, Jiang & Jain 2020; Bondevik et al. 2024) and by health-recommender reviews; the two reviews closest to this system are the diabetes-specific systematic review of Yera et al. (2023) and the 2026 JMIR scoping review of diet recommenders for chronic conditions (Dong et al.), which found only 15 eligible systems, no RCTs, and accuracy-dominated evaluation.

  - Conditions: diabetes 9/15 (60%), hypertension 6 (40%), cancer 3, obesity 3, kidney disease 3 (20% each), high cholesterol 2 (13.3%), iron deficiency 1 (6.7%); target users were single-disease in 6, "complex disease" in 4, mixed healthy and patients in 5 — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/)

### Inferences
- For a chronic-disease paper, S1 and S2 are the most useful anchor citations: both are recent, systematic, and explicitly name the gaps this system can claim to address (multi-condition handling, guideline grounding, clinician involvement) and the gaps it shares (no clinical evaluation).
- S1's finding that 4 of 5 offline evaluations reused training data for testing is directly relevant: a reviewer will check whether this system's held-out "patient types" split avoids that problem.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Bondevik et al. (2024), Tran et al. (2018) and Min et al. (2020) were not read; only abstracts. Their detailed counts (e.g., share of offline evaluations) are not in these notes.
- I found no review dedicated to Bangladesh or South Asia food recommenders.

## Which systems are closest to this one? (ranked list of 18 systems with full records)

### Takeaway
The closest prior systems are DIETOS (clinician-designed guideline flowcharts for CKD, hypertension and diabetes, a regional-food catalogue with per-disease "recommended / moderate / not recommended" labels applied as a filter, tested with 20 CKD patients), PROTEIN AI Advisor (a knowledge-based rule gate followed by meal-plan optimisation, evaluated on 3,000 virtual users), and Yera Toledo et al. (a multicriteria pre-filter that removes unsuitable foods per user type before an integer-programming meal plan, evaluated on 50 synthetic users). None combines a deterministic contraindication gate, learned ranking, ingredient-computed nutrition for a South Asian dish set, sodium budgeting of cooking salt and mandatory physician approval of every plan.

- A closely similar design is Manoharan JS, Sathesh A. "Patient Diet Recommendation System Using K Clique and Deep learning Classifiers." J. Artificial Intelligence and Capsule Networks 2(2):121–130 (2020): GRU with k-clique clustering on 50 patients with 13 features and 1,000 products with 8 features — [Crossref/OpenAlex](https://doi.org/10.36548/jaicn.2020.2.005)
- Similar: learned suitability per patient–food pair (conceptually like this system's trained rankers). Different: the learned model is itself the safety decision; no deterministic gate.

#### 18. Elsweiler & Harvey — algorithmic daily meal plans that meet international guidelines
- Elsweiler D, Harvey M. "Towards Automatic Meal Plan Recommendations for Balanced Nutrition." RecSys 2015, pp. 313–316. DOI 10.1145/2792838.2799665. Conference (short paper) — [Crossref/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1145/2792838.2799665)

### Inferences
- Ranks 1–3 share this system's two-stage shape (exclude unsuitable items first, then rank or optimise). Ranks 4 and 16 share the learned-model element; ranks 5, 10 and 13 show the range of clinician roles (prescription input, expert validation, physician decision only for insulin).
- The two learned-classifier systems (rank 16) are useful foils: a "not-allowed" recall of 73% means about a quarter of unsuitable items were classified as allowed. That supports this system's choice to keep safety in deterministic rules and let models only re-rank what the gate allows. Their numbers come from different data, so they are an argument about design, not a benchmark.

## The five closest systems in depth

### Takeaway
All five closest systems separate "what is allowed" from "what is preferred". Only DIETOS and PROTEIN cover several chronic conditions with clinician- or expert-defined knowledge. Only DIETOS was tested with real patients. PROTEIN, Yera Toledo et al. and Papastratis et al. were evaluated on synthetic or virtual users, the same status as this system. None plans around a cooking-salt sodium budget, uses a South Asian dish table, or requires clinician approval of each plan.

### Cited Findings

#### 1. DIETOS (Agapito et al. 2016, 2017, 2018) — closest overall
- What it built: a web recommender that profiles healthy users and patients with CKD, hypertension and/or diabetes and recommends typical Calabrian foods compatible with their health status — [HealthRecSys 2017 paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- Clinical logic: profiling questionnaires implement guideline flowcharts supplied by the group's medical specialists and nephrologists "accomplishing to World Health Organization and KDOQI guidelines"; three flowcharts profile diabetes, hypertension and CKD in sequence; users enter clinical measurements (creatinine, blood glucose, blood pressure); CKD users get an eGFR estimate by the CKD-EPI formula for staging — [HealthRecSys 2017 paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- Example rule chain (CKD flowchart, Fig. 2): with eGFR > 80 ml/min, a user already classed as hypertensive or diabetic gets a low-calorie, low-salt diet plus physical activity. Otherwise the chart branches on eGFR > 20 ml/min and dialysis status to diets labelled "low-protein, low salt and hypophosphate", the same "and water restriction", "low salt and hypophosphate diet, with a high content of proteins and vitamins", or to "Please contact a Nephrologist". The exact band-to-diet mapping is hard to read from the extracted figure text, so check the figure before quoting it — [HealthRecSys 2017 paper, Fig. 2](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- Food data: a "Typical Food Nutraceutical Table" of Calabrian PDO/PGI foods with nutrition facts per 100 g and, for each food, the conditions for which it is Recommended, Use moderately or Not recommended; pathologies coded with ICD-9-CM — [HealthRecSys 2017 paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- Recommendation: a DIETOSFoodsFilter applies "content selection", keeping only foods labelled Recommended or Use moderately for the user's profile and showing a daily quantity; recommendation is based on health characteristics, not past behaviour; preference learning was future work — [HealthRecSys 2017 paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- Evaluation: 20 CKD patients from the Nephrology and Dialysis Department, Catanzaro University Hospital, and 20 age-matched healthy controls; usability validated with patients and specialists; profiling and recommendation correctness reported as "high specificity and sensitivity" without numbers in the abstract; the online version is "not intended to be used by general users" — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29157465)
- Venue/type: CMPB journal (2018), HealthRecSys workshop at RecSys (2017), IEEE WiMob conference (2016) — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2017.10.014)
- Similar to this system: multiple chronic conditions with lab-value inputs; guideline-derived deterministic logic built with clinicians; per-condition contraindication labels applied as a filter before anything is shown; regional typical foods; salt restriction for hypertension/CKD.
- Differences and gaps this system fills: DIETOS recommends single foods, not dishes with ingredient-computed nutrition; no weekly plan or quantitative sodium budget; no learned ranking; no frequency/familiarity weighting; patient self-profiles via questionnaire instead of doctor entry and approval. Gap this system does not fill: DIETOS was tested with 20 real CKD patients and clinicians; this system has none.

#### 2. PROTEIN AI Advisor (Stefanidis et al. 2022)
- What it built: a two-layer advisor for ten user groups — healthy adolescents, adults and older adults; excess weight; athletes; obesity; cardiovascular disease; type 2 diabetes; iron deficiency; low fruit and vegetable intake — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)
- Layer 1 (qualitative gate): a Reasoning-based Decision Support System using fuzzy description-logic inference (LiFR reasoner) over the NAct ontology of rules from nutrition experts; it rejects meals with foods that conflict with the profile (allergens, condition-restricted foods) and promotes meals with nutrients the user needs — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/); [abstract](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14204435)
- Layer 2 (quantitative planner): random combination of approved meals into six daily slots scored by a fitness function (distance to target energy, fit to nutrient ranges for energy, carbohydrate, fat, saturated fat, protein, fibre, fruit and vegetables, based on EFSA and WHO guidance); constraints classed essential/desirable/non-essential; variety filters limit repeats within and across plans — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)
- Data: more than 2,000 consolidated expert-created meals and 381 expert-built 24-hour plans; nutrients computed with the Nutrium dashboard, with McCance and Widdowson tables for UK plans — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)
- Evaluation (synthetic only): 200 candidate meals checked for appropriateness; 3,000 virtual users with weekly plans; 300 virtual users for profile-complexity tests — [abstract](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14204435); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)
- Key numbers: meal-appropriateness precision 1.000 for every condition/diet, average recall 0.989 (T2D 0.990), F 0.995; meal-plan macronutrient accuracy 92.65% overall (CVD 81.36%, T2D 97.58%, iron deficiency 95.55%, athletes 82.11%); energy difference 7.32% ± 13.08% (r = 0.983); 3–4 different meals per meal type per week; limitations include a meal database too small for high-energy and complex profiles and no real users — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)
- Similar: the same architecture as this system's gate-then-rank (expert rule gate, then nutrient-target plan construction); multi-condition including T2D, CVD, iron deficiency, obesity; weekly plans; synthetic-user evaluation with accuracy against nutrient targets.
- Differences: European expert-composed meals rather than a national household dish set computed from ingredients; no clinician approval step; optimisation by random search with a fitness score rather than learned ranking; no explicit sodium budget (sodium not in the listed constraint set); no CKD or pregnancy groups. Gap shared with this system: no real users.

#### 3. Yera Toledo, Alzahrani & Martínez (2019)
- What it built: a daily meal-plan recommender that combines nutritional and preference information — [IEEE Access](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)
- Data: food profiles from "two popular food composition tables" (600+ foods in 12 groups, per 100 g); a nutritionist set realistic portions and their nutrient amounts; foods described by protein, lipids, carbohydrates, cholesterol, sodium and saturated fat; menu templates (breakfast, lunch, dinner) defined with a nutrition expert — [IEEE Access full text](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)
- Gate: an AHPSort multicriteria pre-filter classifies each food as appropriate or inappropriate against a "limiting profile" for four user types (overweight, diabetic, hypertensive, healthy). Criteria weights from expert pairwise comparison: sodium 0.3562, saturated fat 0.3249, protein 0.1937, cholesterol 0.1250 (consistency ratio 0.016). Rules used in the case study include saturated fat < 7% of energy and cholesterol < 200 mg for diabetics, sodium < 2,500 mg/day for hypertensives, and for everyone cholesterol < 350 mg/day and sodium < 3,000 mg/day. Of 582 foods, 32 were excluded for overweight users, 40 for diabetics, 1 (salad cod) for hypertensives and none for healthy users. Excluded foods "are not considered as candidate items for the next recommendation step" — [IEEE Access full text](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)
- Planner: a binary decision vector over foods (food in menu or not) maximising the sum of per-food weights subject to daily nutrient requirements, adapted from a traditional diet-planning scheme (Anderson & Earle). Weights come from the user's preference, how often a food was eaten and how recently ("frequency-based menu generation", probabilistic refining, restricted re-generation). Meals take 15%/45%/40% of daily intake; energy from a BMR equation times an activity factor — [IEEE Access full text](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)
- Evaluation: 50 synthetic user profiles (60–80 kg, 160–180 cm, 25–60 years), each with 10 synthetic past daily plans. The model was compared with "traditional" optimisation that maximises a macronutrient and achieved higher average preference. Real-user experiments were explicitly left to future work — [IEEE Access full text](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)
- Similar: an explicit pre-filter gate before optimisation; condition-specific sodium and fat thresholds; composition-table foods; a frequency-of-consumption term in the objective (an individual-level counterpart of this system's household cooking-frequency weighting); synthetic users.
- Differences: single foods, not dishes; one day, not a week; four broad user types instead of eight clinical conditions; the gate is a weighted multicriteria score (so one criterion can trade off against another) rather than hard contraindication rules; no clinician approval; no learned ranker.

#### 4. Papastratis, Konstantinidis, Daras & Dimitropoulos (2024)
- What it built: a weekly meal-plan generator in which a variational autoencoder encodes the user profile (weight, height, BMR, age, BMI, target energy, physical activity level, and yes/no flags for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and iron deficiency); a GRU decoder emits six meals per day; an optimiser then adjusts quantities to energy needs — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11199627/); [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x)
- Guidelines: loss functions penalise deviations from EFSA/WHO energy and macronutrient ranges, "to align the network with established nutritional guidelines"; ChatGPT expands the meal pool with equivalent meals from various cuisines (base database: Protein NAP) — [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11199627/)
- Evaluation: 3,000 virtual profiles (84,000 daily plans) and 1,000 real profiles (7,000 daily plans), assessed on energy and nutrient accuracy; the abstract describes no user or expert study — [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x)
- Similar: a learned model trained to respect guideline targets (analogous to this system's models learning a guideline-based suitability function); weekly plans; synthetic-profile evaluation; multi-condition flags including iron deficiency.
- Differences: guidelines are soft penalties inside a neural model, with no separate hard safety gate (I did not find an exclusion step in the text read); meal content partly generated by an LLM, whereas this system keeps clinical logic free of LLMs; no clinician approval; no sodium/CKD/pregnancy handling stated in the text read.

#### 5. PREFer (Bianchini et al. 2015, 2017)
- What it built: a menu generator giving "personalized and healthy menus, taking into account both user's short/long-term preferences and medical prescriptions", with "prescription types associated with users' profiles" used to improve food choices (Semantic Scholar TLDR of the CSI article) — [Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csi.2016.10.010)
- Method (from the repository abstract as surfaced in search; the full text could not be opened): recipes selected by content-based retrieval comparing features annotating user profiles and recipes; menus built from the selected recipes; menus ranked "taking into account also prescription schemes"; reference prescription schemes defined to guide suggestions; developed in a Lombardy regional project linked to Expo 2015 Milan — [UniBS repository record](https://iris.unibs.it/handle/11379/485180) (403 to automated fetch; content seen via search snippet)
- Earlier version: semantic-driven food recommendation "with reference prescriptions", WISE 2015 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-26187-4_3)
- Similar: a clinical prescription is an explicit input that constrains what menus are offered, the closest analogue to this system's physician-set targets; recipes and menus rather than single foods.
- Differences: prescriptions shape ranking rather than acting as a hard pre-ranking gate (as far as the abstract shows); no approval of the output; Italian recipes; evaluation design not verified.

### Inferences
- The closest precedent for this system's safety design is DIETOS's per-condition food labels plus filter. The closest precedents for its learning and evaluation design are PROTEIN and Papastratis et al., which come from the same CERTH group and use virtual-user evaluation against guideline targets. A reviewer will probably ask for at least the level of evaluation those papers report: plan-level nutrient accuracy against targets, variety, and per-condition appropriateness precision/recall.
- Yera Toledo et al.'s weighted AHPSort gate allows compensation between criteria. This system's hard contraindication gate does not, which is a defensible and citable design difference.

### Gaps
- The DIETOS CMPB full text and PREFer full text were not accessible; the DIETOS sensitivity/specificity values and PREFer's evaluation remain unverified.
- For Papastratis et al., the part of the text I could read (about the first 100k characters) did not show the exclusion handling or the metric values.

## Which systems target diabetes, hypertension (DASH, sodium), CKD, anaemia/iron deficiency, pregnancy or high cholesterol — and which handle several at once?

### Takeaway
Diabetes dominates. Hypertension appears mostly as a sodium threshold or a DASH label. CKD has a few rule-based systems (DIETOS, Chi et al.) plus recent LLM evaluations. Iron deficiency and anaemia appear only as one user group or flag (PROTEIN, Papastratis, Diet-Right, a pregnancy ontology), and pregnancy is rare (Sinedie for gestational diabetes; Fadillah & Baizal). Multi-condition systems exist (DIETOS, PROTEIN, Diet-Right, Chen et al. 2015, Manoharan & Sathesh) but none covers this system's eight conditions together with pregnancy and CKD.

### Inferences
- No verified system covers pregnancy, CKD, anaemia and high cholesterol together with diabetes, hypertension and obesity, and none treats knee osteoarthritis in a diet recommender. This system's condition coverage is a defensible novelty claim, as long as it is framed as coverage, not as validated performance per condition.
- Sodium handling in the literature is a per-food or per-day threshold (Yera Toledo et al.) or a "low-salt" label (DIETOS). I found no recommender that budgets added cooking salt across a week's meals. Maillot & Drewnowski's infeasibility result is a useful citation for why a 2,000 mg/day ceiling needs a planning mechanism, not just a filter.

### Gaps
- No dedicated, peer-reviewed anaemia/iron-deficiency diet recommender was found (one 2026 Frontiers in AI anaemia paper appeared in search, but I did not open or verify it).
- Kairat et al.'s and Diet-Right's full results were not read.

## Which approaches are used, and which filter unsafe items before ranking?

### Takeaway
Knowledge-based, ontology and rule systems (often with SWRL or fuzzy reasoning) dominate the clinical end. Hybrid systems dominate the reviews' counts. Learned ranking appears mainly as collaborative filtering, graph neural networks or deep classifiers on recipe-site data. Explicit "filter first, then rank or optimise" pipelines exist (DIETOS, PROTEIN, Yera Toledo, Yum-me), but post-filtering or soft penalties are at least as common, and some systems let the learned model make the safety decision itself.

### Cited Findings
- Review counts: hybrid 40% and knowledge-based 32% of nutrition recommenders — [Abhari et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.31661/jbpe.v0i0.1248); hybrid 86.7% by technology in chronic-condition systems — [Dong et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/); semantic, optimisation, rules/classification and interaction families for diabetes — [Yera et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054248); "the majority of HRSs use hybrid recommendation algorithms" — [De Croon et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/18035)
- Ontology/rules: OWL+SWRL — [Chi et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbi.2014.11.001), [Fadillah & Baizal 2024](https://doi.org/10.33395/sinkron.v8i3.13722); ontology + decision tree + Jena — [Chen et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAAI.2015.7407127); fuzzy ontology — [Lee et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/TFUZZ.2010.2042454); fuzzy DL rule base — [PROTEIN](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14204435); ontology for older adults — [HeNuALs](https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics10172129)
- Knowledge graphs: guideline constraints in KBQA — [Chen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3437963.3441816); dish–ingredient–nutrient–disease graph — [Xu et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52170); attention over heterogeneous recipe graphs on Allrecipes — [Forouzandeh et al. 2024, Comput Biol Med 169:107882](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compbiomed.2023.107882)
- Collaborative and content-based filtering: weekly plans via collaborative filtering then health-history adjustment — [Zioutos, Kondylakis, Stefanidis, Computers 13(1):1](https://doi.org/10.3390/computers13010001); SVD CF with health post-filter — [Starke et al. 2021](https://dars.uib.no/pubs/MORS_RecSys2021.pdf); content-based + prescription — [PREFer](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csi.2016.10.010); knowledge-aware holistic user models — [Musto, Trattner, Starke, Semeraro, UMAP 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3340631.3394880)
- Filter before ranking (gate): DIETOS keeps only Recommended/Use-moderately foods — [CEUR](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf); PROTEIN's RDSS rejects conflicting meals before plan synthesis — [PMC9612332](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/); Yera Toledo's AHPSort pre-filter discards inappropriate foods before optimisation — [IEEE Access](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413); Yum-me "projects the learned profile into the domain of nutritionally appropriate food options", i.e., preference learning over a nutrient-filtered candidate set — [Yang et al., ACM TOIS 36(1):1–31 (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3072614); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07722)
- Filter after ranking (post-filter) or soft penalties: post-filtering tempers the tendency of algorithms to promote popular unhealthy recipes — [Trattner & Elsweiler, WWW 2017, pp. 489–498](https://doi.org/10.1145/3038912.3052573); Cholesterol Factor re-weighting — [Starke et al. 2021](https://dars.uib.no/pubs/MORS_RecSys2021.pdf); guideline-aligned loss functions — [Papastratis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x)
- Learned model as the safety decision: allowed/not-allowed classifiers with 73% recall on the not-allowed class — [Iwendi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2020.2968537); [Manoharan & Sathesh 2020](https://doi.org/10.36548/jaicn.2020.2.005)
- Monotonic constraints: a targeted search found only general documentation of monotonic constraints in gradient boosting (XGBoost, scikit-learn), and no food or diet recommender using them — [XGBoost docs](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/monotonic.html); [scikit-learn example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/ensemble/plot_monotonic_constraints.html)

### Inferences
- This system's combination (hard deterministic gate, then a learned re-ranker that cannot resurrect gated items, with monotonic constraints and a deterministic fallback) is not found in the verified literature as a whole. Each part has precedents: gates in DIETOS, PROTEIN and Yera Toledo; learned ranking in many CF/GNN systems; guideline-aligned learning in Papastratis.
- Monotonic constraints are a credible contribution to argue as "clinically sensible direction of effect". But the claim rests on one negative search, so word it as "we found no prior food recommender that ...".

### Gaps
- No systematic search was run for monotonic/shape-constrained models in health recommenders beyond one query; a wider search (e.g., "shape constraints clinical risk models") was not done.

## Which papers treat meal planning as optimisation (diet problem, LP/IP, multi-objective, weekly plans, sodium or nutrient constraints)?

### Takeaway
Meal planning as optimisation runs from Stigler's 1945 diet problem through a large LP literature in nutrition science (reviews by van Dooren and Gazan et al.) to recommender-side integer programs and metaheuristics. Acceptability, meaning what people actually eat, is the recognised weak point of LP diets. Weekly or multi-day plans appear in PROTEIN, Papastratis, Zioutos and Benvenuti & De Santis.

### Cited Findings
- Seminal diet problem: Stigler GJ. "The Cost of Subsistence." Journal of Farm Economics 27(2):303 (1945) — [Crossref/JSTOR DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1231810)
- LP review: 52 papers; constraints evolved from nutrition to cost, acceptability and ecology; LP shows weaknesses with few food items or nutrient constraints; "no study has provided the ultimate solution to calculating acceptability" — [van Dooren 2018, Front Nutr 5:48](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2018.00048)
- Optimisation review: 67 studies; diet optimisation finds food combinations meeting constraints while minimising or maximising an objective; infeasible constraints signal incompatibility between nutrient recommendations, monotonous consumption patterns or inadequate supply — [Gazan et al. 2018, Adv Nutr 9(5):602–616](https://doi.org/10.1093/advances/nmy049)
- Dish-level multi-day menus: binary integer LP gives "the sequence and composition of daily meals over a given period", built from a given set of dishes whose energy, nutrients, price and environmental impact are calculated, with acceptability handled explicitly (schools, nursing homes) — [Benvenuti & De Santis 2020, Front Nutr 7:562833](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2020.562833)
- Recommender-side programs: binary IP maximising preference under nutrient constraints — [Yera Toledo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413); fitness-function search over expert meals for weekly plans — [PROTEIN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/); VAE/GRU plus quantity optimiser for weekly plans — [Papastratis](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x); ant colony optimisation over pathological-report constraints — [Diet-Right](https://doi.org/10.3837/tiis.2017.06.006); bin-packing menu planning for breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks under energy requirements (BRAC University authors; EEG-derived food affect plus TOPSIS ranking) — [Islam et al., IEEE TCSS 10(4):2099–2110 (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCSS.2022.3213506); feasibility of guideline-meeting daily plans — [Elsweiler & Harvey 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2792838.2799665); collaborative-filtering weekly plans — [Zioutos et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/computers13010001)
- Sodium as a hard constraint: 1,500 mg/day infeasible and 2,300 mg/day feasible only with large shifts in US modelled diets — [Maillot & Drewnowski 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2011.10.009)
- LP with local foods in Bangladesh: Cost of the Diet software (LP over local market prices; focus groups for habits; per-meal constraints consistent with typical habits) — [Deptford et al. 2017, BMC Nutrition 3:26](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-017-0136-4); LP to design rice-lentil and chickpea ready-to-use supplementary food from local ingredients, tested in 90 Dhaka children — [Ahmed et al. 2014, BMC Pediatrics 14:164](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2431-14-164); LP pregnancy diet from Bangladeshi foods — [Shaheen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0296831)
- Early computer menu planning: Seljak BK. "Computer-based dietary menu planning." J. Food Composition and Analysis 22(5):414–420 (2009) — metadata verified only — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2009.02.006)

### Inferences
- This system's weekly plan with cooking-salt rationing is a constrained-planning method. It should cite the diet-problem/LP line and explain why it uses a ration or heuristic instead of an exact LP/IP. Reviewers from nutrition science will expect a comparison with, or at least a justification against, an LP/IP formulation (e.g., Benvenuti & De Santis for dish-level weekly menus).
- The acceptability problem named by van Dooren is exactly what the household-cooking-frequency weighting targets, so citing it frames that weighting as an answer to a known gap.

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether the Cost of the Diet paper's examples use Bangladeshi data (a search summary suggested related Bangladesh work, but the paper's abstract is generic).

## Which use food composition tables and compute recipe nutrition from ingredients? Which model cultural familiarity, popularity or cooking frequency?

### Takeaway
National food composition tables are used in a minority of systems (e.g., Taiwanese foods in Lee et al.; UK McCance & Widdowson in PROTEIN; two composition tables in Yera Toledo et al.; Thai, UK and Japanese sources in the 2026 review). Most recipe recommenders rely on recipe-site nutrition (AllRecipes, Yummly, Recipe1M+). Cultural fit is handled by regional catalogues or recipe-based plans. Popularity is mostly treated as a bias toward unhealthy items. Consumption frequency appears at the individual level (Yera Toledo) or as co-consumption in national survey data (Vandeputte et al.). I found no recommender that weights dishes by how often households cook them.

### Cited Findings
- Composition tables: 5/15 chronic-condition systems used authoritative government/institutional databases — [Dong et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/); Malaysian, Indonesian and USDA tables in diabetes systems — [Yera et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054248); two composition tables (600+ foods) with nutritionist-set portions — [Yera Toledo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413); Nutrium and McCance & Widdowson — [PROTEIN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/); expert-created nutrition facts of common Taiwanese foods — [Lee et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/TFUZZ.2010.2042454); Calabrian PDO/PGI food table — [DIETOS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- Recipe nutrition: NutRec builds a "healthy pseudo-recipe" with ingredient amounts and retrieves similar recipes (Allrecipes 36,429 and Yummly 89,413 recipes) — [Chen M et al. 2020, Information Processing & Management 57(6):102051](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2019.05.012); AllRecipes healthiness assessed against WHO and UK FSA standards — [Trattner & Elsweiler 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3038912.3052573); dish nutrient content calculated for menu LP — [Benvenuti & De Santis 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2020.562833); large recipe/image corpus (1M+ recipes, 13M images) — [Marin et al., Recipe1M+, IEEE TPAMI 43(1):187–203 (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2019.2927476)
- Closest Bangladeshi data resource (not peer-reviewed; dataset): FoodNetBD, 600 Bangladeshi foods, 33 nutrients per 100 g, English–Bengali names, safe/restrict/avoid tags across 12 conditions (incl. type 2 diabetes, hypertension, CKD, anaemia), compiled from the Food Composition Table for Bangladesh, Indian Food Composition Tables 2017 and USDA FoodData Central (KUET and NUBTK, Khulna) — [Suma, Hasan, Shafi, Mendeley Data 2026, DOI 10.17632/23f7yxnvy7](https://doi.org/10.17632/23f7yxnvy7)
- Other Bangladeshi food-recommendation work: fuzzy-logic "income based food list" for rural people using BMI, age, recommended nutrients and income, with local foods split into low- and high-cost groups (North South University) — [Hussain et al., IEEE/ACIS ICIS 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICIS.2018.8466403); CKD potassium-level foods (East Delta University) — [Banerjee et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICCCI.2019.8821871); menu planning with EEG affect (BRAC University; food set not verified) — [Islam et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCSS.2022.3213506)
- Cultural familiarity: plans from recipes "rather than individual food items, limiting recommendations to tasteful and culturally acceptable food combinations" — [Ribeiro et al., SousChef validation study, Applied Sciences 12(2):702 (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3390/app12020702); earlier SousChef (older adults, nutritionist guidelines; more than 70% of older-adult testers satisfied) — [ICT4AWE 2017, pp. 36–45](https://doi.org/10.5220/0006281900360045); [CCIS 2018, pp. 107–126](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-93644-4_6); socioeconomic, cultural and geographical tailoring — [Alian et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2018.2882138); Mauritian diet plans — [Sookrah et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICoICT.2019.8835323); Chinese recipe websites + eating history — [Xu et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52170); Thai dishes with DNN — [Mokdara et al., ICT-ISPC 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICT-ISPC.2018.8523950)
- Popularity: users interact most with the least healthy recipes, and recommender algorithms "tend to score popular items highly and thus on average promote unhealthy items" — [Trattner & Elsweiler, WWW 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3038912.3052573); popularity bias "at odds with the objective of healthy recipe recommendation" — [Starke et al. 2021](https://dars.uib.no/pubs/MORS_RecSys2021.pdf); visual cues bias choice, and nudging via substitution tested in user studies (n=107, 111, 138) — [Elsweiler, Trattner, Harvey, SIGIR 2017, pp. 575–584](https://doi.org/10.1145/3077136.3080826)
- Consumption frequency/acceptability: preference weights from past frequency and recency — [Yera Toledo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413); association rules between dishes eaten in the same meal, mined from a large food consumption database to propose acceptable substitutions (255 adults judged plausibility; 27 volunteers in an app test; higher acceptance when users helped choose, OR = 3.168) — [Vandeputte et al. 2023, J Nutr 153(2):598–604](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2022.12.022); [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36894251); acceptability constraints in LP — [van Dooren 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2018.00048)

### Inferences
- Computing 250 dish profiles from ingredients with FCTB/IFCT/USDA values sits between the recipe-site approach and expert-composed meals (PROTEIN). Reviewers will ask how cooking losses, yields and oil absorption were handled, since composition-based recipe nutrition depends on them.
- FoodNetBD (2026) overlaps this system's food-table construction (same three sources, Bangladeshi foods, condition tags). The paper should cite it and say what differs, for example the recipe layer, the gate rules with guideline citations, and the corrections log. Otherwise a reviewer may question novelty.
- Weighting by household cooking frequency is closest to Vandeputte et al.'s consumption-data acceptability and Yera Toledo's frequency term. The popularity–unhealthiness finding means the paper should show that frequency weighting cannot override health ranking or the gate.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed recommender that uses the Bangladesh FCT (FCTB 2013) or Bangladeshi recipes computed from ingredients; this is a negative search result, not proof of absence.

## How were these systems evaluated, and what evaluation do reviewers expect?

### Takeaway
Most systems are evaluated offline or on synthetic/virtual users, sometimes with a small expert agreement check. Real-patient studies are rare and small (20–96 people), and RCTs almost absent: one gestational-diabetes CDSS ran one, and the 2026 scoping review found none. Reviews ask for clear reporting (De Croon's five items), beyond-accuracy and health metrics, expert validation, real-world and longitudinal studies, and separation of training and test data.

### Cited Findings
- Accuracy-dominated, offline, train/test leakage: [Dong et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/); half of HRS studies evaluated only the algorithm — [De Croon et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/18035); offline dominance and no published full-online food RS evaluations (as of 2017) — [Trattner & Elsweiler preprint](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.02760); "a distinct lack of robust scientific research", heterogeneous outcomes — [Ananthakrishnan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2024.105697); only 19.6% of HRSs considered end-user preferences in design — [Sun et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2196/38184)
- Synthetic/virtual users: 3,000 virtual users — [PROTEIN](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14204435); 3,000 virtual + 1,000 real profiles — [Papastratis](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x); 50 synthetic profiles — [Yera Toledo](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413); 100 profiles — [Elsweiler & Harvey](https://doi.org/10.1145/2792838.2799665); Monte Carlo virtual dialysis patient plus a renal dietitian's rating — [Wang et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jrn.2024.09.001); simulated CKD-stage profiles rated by three physicians — [Kairat et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm14228033); 5 of 8 diabetes optimisation works used simulated scenarios — [Yera et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054248)
- Expert agreement: three nutrition specialists — [Norouzi et al.](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30415555); dietitians, "100%" — [Chen et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAAI.2015.7407127); nutritionists — [Phanich et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICISA.2010.5480416); human expert verification — [Alian et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2018.2882138); domain-expert diet validation — [Lee et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/TFUZZ.2010.2042454)
- Real users/patients: 20 CKD patients + 20 controls — [DIETOS](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29157465); 90 gestational-diabetes patients over 17 months, RCT design — [Sinedie](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28495347); 30 diabetic patients by questionnaire — [Norouzi](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30415555); 96 older adults' data, effects assessed by simulation over 30 days — [ElCombo](https://doi.org/10.2196/52170); field study of 227 users and a 60-person user study with 42.63% higher acceptance — [Yum-me](https://doi.org/10.1145/3072614); usability and satisfaction with older adults — [SousChef 2017](https://doi.org/10.5220/0006281900360045)
- Reporting expectations: state who the target user is, what is recommended and how it is presented, where the dataset is, which algorithm, and the evaluation protocol — [De Croon et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/18035); standard datasets/baselines, online studies, beyond-accuracy metrics (diversity), health metrics — [Trattner & Elsweiler](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.02760); assessment of "scientific soundness" of recommendations, long-term and clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness — [Dong et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/)

### Inferences
- Within this literature, this system's offline synthetic evaluation is normal for a design paper (PROTEIN, Yera Toledo, Papastratis are comparable). The cheapest step that would move it up a tier is an expert-agreement study: physicians or dietitians rating a sample of gated, ranked plans (as in Norouzi, Chen 2015, Wang 2024, Kairat 2025).
- Reviewers will distinguish "the model recovers the labelling function" from "recommendations are appropriate". PROTEIN and Papastratis report nutrient-target accuracy and per-condition appropriateness precision/recall. Equivalent metrics for this system (e.g., plan sodium vs 2,000 mg, energy vs target, gate recall on a hand-labelled contraindication set) would align it with the field. Their published numbers cannot be compared directly because the data differ.

### Gaps
- I did not find a field-specific evaluation standard for clinical diet recommenders beyond the general HRS reporting guidelines.

## Synthesis: what is common, what gaps this system addresses, and what a reviewer would raise

### Takeaway
The field mostly builds single-condition (usually diabetes), Western- or East Asian-cuisine systems with knowledge-based or hybrid methods, evaluated offline or on synthetic users. This system's verifiable novelty lies in the combination: Bangladeshi dishes with ingredient-computed nutrition, eight conditions including pregnancy, CKD and anaemia, a hard guideline-cited gate before learned ranking with monotonic constraints, a week plan with a cooking-salt sodium budget, and physician approval of every plan. Its main weakness, shared with PROTEIN, Yera Toledo et al. and Papastratis et al., is that there is no real-patient or clinician evaluation, and its models learn a hand-written synthetic labelling function.

### Cited Findings
- Common pattern: hybrid/knowledge-based methods and accuracy-centred, mostly offline evaluation — [Dong et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/); [De Croon et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/18035); [Yera et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054248)
- Gate-then-rank precedents: [DIETOS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf); [PROTEIN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/); [Yera Toledo](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413); [Yum-me](https://doi.org/10.1145/3072614)
- Clinician roles in prior systems: prescription as input — [PREFer](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csi.2016.10.010); expert validation of menus — [Lee et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/TFUZZ.2010.2042454); physicians decide only insulin while diet advice goes straight to patients — [Sinedie](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28495347); clinician app with permissions — [HeNuALs](https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics10172129); medical-team-designed profiling — [DIETOS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)
- LLM-generated diets show large nutrient-estimate errors for renal nutrients and need expert checking — [Wang et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jrn.2024.09.001); [Kairat et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm14228033)
- Learned safety classifiers miss unsafe items (not-allowed recall 73%) — [Iwendi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2020.2968537)

### Inferences
What the system adds relative to the verified literature:
- **Local cuisine at dish level with computed nutrition.** Prior regional systems use single typical foods (DIETOS), expert-composed meals (PROTEIN) or national diet plans without a stated composition pipeline (Sookrah). I found no verified recommender using Bangladeshi household dishes whose nutrition is computed from FCTB/IFCT/USDA ingredients. FoodNetBD is a dataset, not a recommender, and must be cited.
- **Many simultaneous conditions, including pregnancy, CKD and anaemia.** The largest verified multi-condition systems (PROTEIN's ten groups, DIETOS's three diseases, Diet-Right) do not combine these.
- **A hard, test-enforced gate before any learned ranking.** Several systems filter first (DIETOS, PROTEIN, Yera Toledo), but the gates are rule labels or weighted multicriteria scores, and others let learned models or soft penalties carry safety (Iwendi, Papastratis). Re-ranking that cannot add gated items, plus monotonic constraints, plus a deterministic fallback, has no verified precedent.
- **Physician approval of every plan.** No verified diet recommender requires sign-off on each output; the nearest are expert validation in research (Lee et al.) and physician decision limited to insulin (Sinedie).
- **Sodium budgeting through cooking salt over a Saturday–Friday week.** Prior work uses per-day sodium thresholds or "low-salt" labels; Maillot & Drewnowski show why strict sodium targets make plans hard to satisfy.
- **Clinical logic kept separate from LLMs.** This contrasts with Papastratis (ChatGPT meal expansion) and the LLM CKD studies.

Weaknesses a reviewer is likely to raise, with the literature that sets the bar:
- **No real patients, clinicians or user study.** DIETOS (20 CKD patients), Sinedie (90 patients, RCT design), Norouzi (3 specialists + 30 patients) and Yum-me (227 + 60 users) all went further. An expert-agreement study is the expected minimum in this subfield.
- **Circular evaluation.** The models are trained and scored on labels from the system's own hand-written suitability function. Reviewers will say Spearman agreement shows fidelity to the labeller, not appropriateness. Add plan-level metrics against external targets (energy, sodium, per-condition contraindication recall on an independently labelled set), as PROTEIN reported.
- **Missing nutrients.** No saturated-fat or cholesterol values, while Yera Toledo's pre-filter and Starke's Cholesterol Factor use them; the high-cholesterol rule therefore relies on dish categories.
- **Preferences and feedback are not learned from patients.** Most hybrids model individual preference or history (Yera Toledo, Yum-me, Zioutos). Household cooking frequency is population-level, and popularity correlates with unhealthiness in recipe data (Trattner & Elsweiler).
- **Optimisation baseline.** Nutrition-science reviewers may ask why a weekly LP/IP (e.g., Benvenuti & De Santis) was not used or compared.
- **Recipe-nutrition accuracy.** Cooking losses, yields and oil uptake in ingredient-computed dishes need to be stated.
- **Novelty against FoodNetBD and DIETOS** must be argued explicitly.
- **Physician approval is a workflow control, not evidence of correctness.** No agreement or override rates exist yet.

### Gaps
- No verified paper was found that combines a Bangladeshi dish database, several chronic conditions and a clinician-in-the-loop recommender. This is a negative finding from a limited search (Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, web search) and should be phrased cautiously in the paper.
- Full texts not read: PREFer, DIETOS (CMPB), Chi et al., Li et al. (JWS), Bondevik et al., Min et al., Tran et al., Kairat et al., Diet-Right, Seljak 2009, Mahajan & Kaur. Claims about them rest on abstracts, TLDRs or companion papers.
