# Prior work matches parts, never the whole

No paper in the six topic notes combines what this project combines, but each of its parts has a close precedent, and several of those precedents were tested more strongly than this project has been. The nearest diet systems are DIETOS, which applies clinician-built guideline flowcharts and per-condition food labels as a filter for kidney disease, hypertension and diabetes and was tried with 20 CKD patients; PROTEIN AI Advisor, whose expert-rule layer screens meals before a weekly-plan optimiser; and Yera Toledo et al., whose multicriteria pre-filter removes unsuitable foods before an integer program. MAI-DIET (2026) is the nearest example of keeping the arithmetic in rules and confining an LLM (Claude) to supervised tasks, FITTER (a preprint) is the nearest exercise prescriber, and Ma et al. (2023) is the nearest lab-slip reader. What the project can defensibly claim as new is the combination: Bangladeshi home dishes whose nutrition is computed from FCTB, IFCT and USDA ingredients; eight conditions including pregnancy, CKD, anaemia and high cholesterol; a test-enforced gate before monotonic learned re-ranking; a weekly cooking-salt budget under 2,000 mg of sodium; physician approval of each plan, with an append-only audit log; and a Bangla interface. That claim should be presented as the result of a bounded, non-systematic search. The project's weakness is evidence. Its patients, labels and lab slips are all synthetic, while DIETOS, Sinedie, MAI-DIET, FITTER, P3-EX and the Bangladeshi trials all involved real clinicians or patients. Three facts in the code also change what the paper can claim. First, the LLM rewrites the report every time it is requested after approval, so a patient can read wording the physician never saw. Second, the hyperparameter search used the same archetype folds as the evaluation, so the 0.998 and 0.984 Spearman figures are probably slightly optimistic until a nested cross-validation is run. Third, the live app ranks with the trained model, not with the label function, so the paper must say why the model exists and where it departs from that function. For the paper, this means a related-work section in four strands plus a regional paragraph, a novelty claim worded as "we found no system that ...", a limitations section that names the phrasing and tuning problems next to the synthetic data, and an evaluation plan led by a blinded physician and dietitian agreement study.

*The verification caveats from the notes appear in square brackets after a paper's first mention: [preprint] means not peer reviewed; [abstract] means the details come from the abstract only; [partial] means the full text was read only in part or through a summarising fetch; [metadata] means only the bibliographic record was confirmed; TODO(verify) marks a detail to recheck before citing it. All papers were checked against a source page on 15–16 November 2026. Facts about the project come from the [README](../../README.md), [CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md) and the code.*

## Twelve papers come closest, and each matches only part of the design

The project has eight defining features: clinical inputs covering several conditions; deterministic rules with guideline citations; a safety gate that runs before ranking; local dishes with food-composition data; weekly plans under nutrient limits; a learned ranker; approval of each patient's plan by a clinician; and an LLM kept out of the clinical logic. It also has exercise and OCR modules. The table ranks the closest papers from all six notes by how many of these features they share, the criterion the notes themselves used.

The table shows a field that already separates what is allowed from what is preferred. DIETOS keeps only foods labelled Recommended or Use moderately for the user's profile ([Agapito, HealthRecSys 2017](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1953/healthRecSys17_paper_13.pdf)). PROTEIN's reasoner rejects meals that conflict with the profile before it builds a plan ([Stefanidis 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)). Yera Toledo et al. state that excluded foods "are not considered as candidate items for the next recommendation step" ([Yera Toledo 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)). So the gate-then-rank shape is not the project's contribution, and a reviewer who knows DIETOS will correct a paper that implies it is. **What no row has is the whole stack at once, and no verified diet recommender requires a clinician to sign off each plan before the patient sees it.** The clinician roles in the literature are narrower: experts validate menus during research (Lee et al.); physicians decide insulin while diet advice reaches patients unreviewed ([Sinedie](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28495347)); physicians fill in a one-off questionnaire, approving about 75% of generated physical-activity recommendations ([Ferretto et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2020.2983564) [abstract]); or clinicians rate plans that no patient ever received (MAI-DIET, FITTER). A prescription can also be an input that shapes menu ranking ([PREFer, Bianchini et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csi.2016.10.010) [full text not opened; method from a repository abstract]).

The safety notes point out that the literature falls into two camps with little overlap, and the project sits in the gap between them. Recommender-systems work constrains learned models with rules but rarely includes clinician approval: PROTEIN, Yera Toledo, and constrained question answering over a food knowledge graph ([Chen et al., WSDM 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3437963.3441816)). Clinical-informatics work studies clinicians reviewing LLM drafts but has no rule-gated diet engine ([Garcia 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38506805/); [Chen 2024](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC11829255); [Zaretsky 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38466307/)). That gap is the honest place to put the project, and it can be stated in one sentence in the introduction.

On evidence, the project belongs in the lowest tier, with PROTEIN, Yera Toledo et al. and Papastratis et al., all of which were evaluated on synthetic or virtual users. The 2026 JMIR scoping review of diet recommenders for chronic conditions screened 4,492 records and kept **15 systems**. It found **no RCTs**, accuracy as the most common criterion (9 of 15), samples of typically 20–40 patients, and dietitian validation in only 3 evaluations ([Dong et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/)). Synthetic-only evaluation is therefore normal for a design paper. It is still below what the closest architectural peers did: DIETOS and Sinedie enrolled patients, MAI-DIET, FITTER and P3-EX put real clinicians in front of their outputs, and in the regional work Ma et al. tested on real lab reports.

## Diet recommenders already filter before ranking, so the gate's hardness is the difference

The gates differ in kind, and the paper's argument should rest on that difference. Yera Toledo et al.'s pre-filter is a weighted multicriteria score. Experts set the weights by pairwise comparison (sodium 0.3562, saturated fat 0.3249, protein 0.1937, cholesterol 0.1250), so one criterion can offset another. The filter excluded 40 of 582 foods for diabetic users and only one (salad cod) for hypertensive users ([Yera Toledo 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2019.2929413)). PROTEIN's layer uses fuzzy description-logic inference over expert rules ([Stefanidis 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)). Other systems filter after ranking, or put guidelines into soft penalties: post-filtering against popular unhealthy recipes ([Trattner & Elsweiler, WWW 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3038912.3052573)), a "Cholesterol Factor" re-weighting ([Starke et al. 2021](https://dars.uib.no/pubs/MORS_RecSys2021.pdf)), and loss functions aligned to guidelines ([Papastratis 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-65438-x)). In some systems the learned model is itself the safety decision. Iwendi et al.'s allowed/not-allowed classifiers reported **recall of 73% on the not-allowed class**, which means about a quarter of unsuitable items were classed as allowed ([Iwendi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2020.2968537) [abstract]). That figure comes from different data, so it supports a design argument and is not a benchmark. Against this background the project's gate is hard (no compensation between criteria), cites a guideline for each rule in `data/reference/contraindications.yaml`, runs before any ranking, and is enforced by `tests/test_contraindication.py`. The trained models only re-rank what the gate allowed, under monotonic constraints, with a deterministic formula as the fallback. The food notes found no food or diet recommender that uses monotonic constraints, after one targeted search ([food notes](notes/food_diet_recommenders.md)). The paper should therefore say "we found no prior food recommender that ..." rather than "the first".

Condition coverage is a fair novelty claim, as long as it is framed as coverage and not as validated performance per condition. Diabetes dominates the field: it appeared in 9 of the 15 chronic-condition systems ([Dong 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/)), and a diabetes-only systematic review found 34 papers ([Yera et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054248)). Hypertension usually appears as a sodium threshold or a DASH label. Iron deficiency appears only as a user group or a flag (PROTEIN, Papastratis, and Diet-Right, [Rehman et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3837/tiis.2017.06.006) [full results not read]). Pregnancy is rare: Sinedie covers gestational diabetes, and an ontology menu system for pregnant women was tested on 15 sample profiles ([Fadillah & Baizal 2024](https://doi.org/10.33395/sinkron.v8i3.13722)). The largest multi-condition systems are PROTEIN (ten groups) and DIETOS (three diseases). **No verified system combines pregnancy, CKD, anaemia and high cholesterol with diabetes, hypertension and obesity, and no diet recommender handles knee osteoarthritis.** One limit belongs next to this claim. The food table has no saturated-fat or cholesterol values, so the high-cholesterol plan excludes solid-fat dishes instead of counting those nutrients. Yera Toledo's pre-filter and Starke's Cholesterol Factor both use exactly those values.

Sodium is where the project's planning method is most clearly new, and also where nutrition-science reviewers will push hardest. The prior work uses either a per-day threshold (sodium under 2,500 mg for hypertensive users in Yera Toledo) or a low-salt label (DIETOS). The notes found no recommender that budgets added cooking salt across a week. Linear programming on US diets found 2,300 mg a day compatible with nutrient adequacy for adults under 50 only with large dietary change, and **1,500 mg a day infeasible across 27 nutrient constraints** ([Maillot & Drewnowski 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2011.10.009)). That result is the citation for why a 2,000 mg ceiling needs a planning mechanism and not just a filter. The same literature brings a predictable objection. Diet optimisation runs from Stigler's diet problem to reviews of 52 and 67 LP studies ([van Dooren 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2018.00048); [Gazan et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/advances/nmy049)), and binary integer programs already build multi-day menus at the dish level ([Benvenuti & De Santis 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2020.562833)). A reviewer will ask why the weekly plan uses a salt ration and heuristics rather than an exact LP or IP. The paper should answer this, or compare against one.

The data layer is where a Bangladeshi reviewer will look for overlap. The closest local resource is FoodNetBD: 600 Bangladeshi foods with 33 nutrients per 100 g and safe/restrict/avoid tags across 12 conditions, compiled from the same three sources (FCTB, IFCT 2017, USDA) ([Suma, Hasan & Shafi 2026](https://doi.org/10.17632/23f7yxnvy7) [dataset, not peer reviewed]). The paper must cite it and say what differs: the 250-dish recipe layer computed from ingredients, gate rules with guideline citations, and the corrections log in `data/food/corrections_log.csv`. India's INDB is the nearest analogue of the dish-computation problem. It pairs IFCT 2017 with 1,014 recipes, and **only 42.62% of its recipe ingredients matched a nutrient retention factor** ([Vijayakumar et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cdnut.2024.103790)). Reviewers will ask how cooking losses, yields and oil uptake were handled. The project can answer part of that: its README says recipe nutrition uses the dataset's FAO/INFOODS method with USDA retention factors and reproduces the source workbook, which `tests/test_recipe_nutrients.py` checks. Yields and oil absorption should still be stated explicitly. Weighting dishes by how often households cook them has no published counterpart. The nearest are association rules between dishes eaten in the same meal ([Vandeputte et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2022.12.022)) and an individual frequency term (Yera Toledo). Both papers frame this as acceptability, the weak point that LP reviews name ([van Dooren 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2018.00048)). The risk is that popular recipes tend to be the least healthy ([Trattner & Elsweiler 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3038912.3052573)). The paper should therefore show that frequency cannot override the gate or the health ranking. The project's design already supports this: the familiarity multiplier only scales a nutritional fit, so a dish that does nothing for the patient's targets is never lifted.

## Oversight research supports the physician gate and exposes the post-approval phrasing

The case for keeping LLMs out of clinical logic is strong, but it has to be argued from the right premise. LLM diet output fails exactly where the project's rules operate. ChatGPT's advice for non-communicable diseases ranged from 55.5% to 73.3% appropriate and became "contradictory or inappropriate" for overlapping conditions ([Ponzo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38398794/) [abstract]). A GPT-4 dialysis menu **underestimated potassium by 49%, phosphorus by 54% and sodium by 53%** ([Wang LC et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39278578/) [abstract; the paper's CIs look odd, TODO(verify) against the full text]). GPT-4 recognised only 60% of low-potassium foods ([Qarajeh 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37887080/) [abstract]). Three LLMs' CKD plans reached 1,165–1,548 kcal against an 1,800 kcal target ([You 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41135437/) [abstract]). LLMs in general "follow neither diagnostic nor treatment guidelines, and cannot interpret laboratory results" ([Hager 2024, Nat Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38965432/) [abstract]). The newest models are much better on energy and macronutrients ([Lawabni 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42401322/) [abstract]), and experts could not tell ChatGPT-4 weight-loss plans from tertiary-centre plans ([Kim 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38577160/) [abstract]). So "LLMs are inaccurate" will not survive review on its own. The argument that holds rests on determinism, reproducibility and auditability, plus the residual safety-critical rates. In a multimorbidity study, the proprietary models fully resolved only 57–89% of guideline conflicts, and the GPT and Claude models, the best on safety, still had safety-critical flags in 5–8% of answers ([Göz 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43030301/) [abstract; scored mostly by an LLM judge panel]). Reproducibility was generally poor across four models planning menus for real cases ([Çalık 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42884656/) [abstract]).

The gate argument has unusually direct support. NutriOrion injects contraindications "as hard negative constraints during synthesis", and still left a **12.1% drug-food interaction violation rate** ([Wu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18650) [preprint]). In FITTER's ablation on its cancer cohort, removing the rule-based audit-and-repair loop cut safety-constraint compliance from 99.0% to 62.0% ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03983) [preprint; partial]). A gate outside the generator, applied before ranking, makes the violation rate zero by construction. The safety notes found no paper that reports a test enforcing that property, so the project's test is a small but real contribution. FITTER also supplies the caution that belongs in the same paragraph: model-assessed compliance was 98.5%, but clinicians rated only **80.6% of reviewed prescriptions safe** ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03983) [preprint]). Rule compliance is not clinical safety, and the paper should say this about its own gate.

Physician approval fits what the authorities ask for, but the evidence does not support treating approval as proof of safety. WHO says humans "should remain in control of health-care systems and medical decisions" ([WHO 2021 news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use) [full PDF not read]). Its 2024 guidance on large multimodal models warns of "false, inaccurate, biased, or incomplete statements" and of automation bias ([WHO 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-01-2024-who-releases-ai-ethics-and-governance-guidance-for-large-multi-modal-models) [news release; the publication page shows a later date, not confirmed]). The EU AI Act requires automatic logs (Art 12), oversight with the ability to "disregard, override or reverse" the output (Art 14), and log retention of at least six months (Art 26(6)) ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)). The FDA's January 2026 guidance treats clinician-facing software whose basis the clinician can review independently differently from software that recommends to patients, which it classes as a device ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download)). None of these governs Bangladesh, so the paper should present them as reference standards, not as compliance claims. The evidence on review itself is sobering. Six oncologists editing GPT-4 drafts produced replies that stayed close to the drafts' content, and the unedited drafts carried a risk of severe harm in **7.1% of 156 survey responses** and of death in 0.6%. The authors name "automation bias and anchoring" ([Chen et al. 2024, Lancet Digit Health](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC11829255)). Radiologists' accuracy fell to 19.8–45.5% when an AI suggestion was wrong, against about 80% when it was right ([Dratsch 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37129490/) [abstract]). LLM drafts saved no time in one study ([Garcia 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38506805/) [abstract]) and added 21.8% to read time in another ([Tai-Seale 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38619840/) [abstract]). Two mitigators of automation bias are "emphasizing user accountability" and "the provision of information versus recommendation" ([Goddard et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21685142/) [abstract]). The project's physician reviews deterministic output that can be traced to a guideline, with cautions on the review screen. That is the information-not-recommendation lever, but it is an argument, not a measured result. An approval rate near 100% would show nothing by itself.

**The first code finding sits exactly here.** [`src/api/routes/report.py` lines 60–68](../../src/api/routes/report.py#L60) call the LLM every time `GET /report/{visit_id}` is requested, after the `Approval` row exists. The physician approves the structured result (`approval.edited_result` or the assessment's result). The patient-facing wording is generated afterwards, is never shown to the physician, and can differ between two requests for the same report. The literature on LLM rewriting shows why this matters. When LLMs turned discharge summaries into patient-friendly text, readability improved sharply (grade level 11.0 to 6.2), but **18 of 100 physician reviews noted safety concerns**, mostly omissions plus some hallucinations ([Zaretsky 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38466307/) [abstract]). GPT-4 patient instructions added inferred recommendations in 2.8% of cases ([Woo et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38778578/) [abstract]). LLM simplification of consent forms lost risk information until clinicians revised it ([Luo et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41922537/) [abstract]). The risk is higher in Bangla. In the low-resource language Haitian Creole, potentially clinically significant errors occurred in **33.3% of ChatGPT translations against 8.3% of professional ones** ([Brewster 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38860299/) [abstract]). GPT-3.5, LLaMA-2 and Claude-2 performed poorly on most Bengali tasks ([Kabir et al., LREC-COLING 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.13173)), and a review of Bangla medical question answering found no clinician-validated benchmarks ([Mahmud et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42953604/) [abstract]). The guarantee in `tests/test_api_gate.py` holds at the endpoint: there is no report without approval. The paper cannot say that patients read only what their physician approved. The design should be fixed, or the gap disclosed. The cleanest fix is to generate the phrased text once at approval time, show it to the physician beside the structured result, store the approved text and serve only that. A weaker alternative is an automated check that every approved item and number survives phrasing, with the deterministic template served on any mismatch. Until clinicians have reviewed LLM Bangla, the template should be the default for Bangla reports.

## Exercise and lab-slip modules each trail one closer, better-tested precedent

Exercise prescription is mostly rule-based in the literature, and the project's per-exercise safety dataset is its clearest addition. EXPERT encodes literature-derived dosing and safety advice for 10 cardiovascular diseases, 5 risk factors and 3 other conditions, at the level of the condition and the prescription ([Hansen et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28420250/) [abstract]). Ontologies and knowledge bases such as EXMO store terms and protocols ([Liu et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39695140/)). The exercise notes found **no system with per-exercise safe/modify/avoid ratings for named conditions**. The project's 408 exercises, rated for pregnancy, hypertension, diabetes, anaemia and knee osteoarthritis from 88 cited sources, therefore have no verified counterpart. No exercise ranker trained on synthetic patient types with guideline-derived labels was found either. The nearest learned approaches classify BMI with LightGBM from real student fitness tests ([Mo et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41826482/) [abstract]) or pick a physical-activity pattern by predicted survival ([Yang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.2196/78492) [Crossref record and abstract; journal page returned 403]). Combined diet-and-exercise systems pair diet rules with generic activity targets, not a prescription. The PROTEIN app is the closest: it recommends meals and exercise for groups including iron-deficiency anaemia, and its pilot RCT with 21 adults raised time in range from 71.8% to 76% but missed its target, with high dropout ([Lalama et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42342244/) [abstract]). One gap a reviewer will press is pre-participation screening. ChatGPT's exercise advice was 90.7% accurate, but **8 of its 15 inaccuracies concerned medical clearance** ([Zaleski et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38206661/) [abstract]). P3-EX builds the ACSM clearance step into its tool ([Chen S et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12268123/) [partial]). The paper must say whether the project screens before prescribing (PAR-Q+ or the ACSM algorithm) or relies on the physician.

The daily checklist and streak should be described as untested design choices. Gamified physical-activity interventions pooled to Hedges g = 0.42, falling to 0.15 in the long term ([Mazeas et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34982715/)). Against non-gamified apps, the gain was 489 steps a day, which the authors judged trivial ([Nishi et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39764571/)). Digital self-monitoring of diet and activity has meta-analytic support in adults with overweight (weight −2.87 kg) ([Berry et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34192411/)), which gives the checklist a mechanism to cite. Streak evidence is thin. The most direct trial, of streak against tally feedback with 7,004 enrolled, is registered as completed with no results posted ([NCT07220044](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07220044)). A qualitative study of run streakers found that many "reported running through injuries" ([Curran et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39439910/)). That finding matters for an exercise streak, and it argues for counting the streak over scheduled tasks only, with rest days never treated as breaks.

The lab-slip reader is a modern, safety-first case of the "OCR plus rules" family, and its nearest precedent tested on real documents. Ma et al. ran PP-OCR on a CPU at 0.78 s per report, rebuilt table rows with layout rules, and extracted every test from **153 real Chinese reports**. Their rule baseline reached F1 0.78 and their CRF 0.86, and they excluded reports whose OCR accuracy was below 30% ([Ma et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10629084/)). Neveditsin et al. compared almost the same engine shortlist the project considered, on real Indian phone photos of clinical reports, all on local hardware. PaddleOCR (PP-OCRv5) had a good character error rate (0.111) but the **lowest numeric accuracy (0.674)** and took 14.14 s per image on a CPU. Compact multimodal LLMs reached 0.927–0.950, but needed 11–67 s per image on an A100 ([Neveditsin et al., EACL 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13523v1) [numbers from arXiv v1, which may differ from the camera-ready version]). The configurations differ from the project's RapidOCR setup at about 1.5 s per photo, so the figures cannot be compared directly. Still, the 0.674 suggests that most of the project's gain from 73.8% to 99.7% comes from its post-OCR rules. Reporting raw OCR numeric accuracy before the rules would show this. On unanswerable questions about report images, GPT-4V gave wrong answers to 54 of 84 entity questions ([Jin et al., RJUA-MedDQA, KDD 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14840v1) [the paper describes its data as both real-world and synthetic; provenance ambiguous]). That is the closest published analogue of the project's rule to leave a field empty rather than guess, and it bears on the optional Claude reader. No reviewed paper describes the project's targeted analytes, look-alike exclusion, mmol/L conversion, plausibility rejection, abstention or PDF text-layer path. A synthetic benchmark with controlled damage has a non-medical precedent in Hegghammer's 18,568 noised page images ([Hegghammer 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42001-021-00149-1) [abstract]). The weaknesses are just as specific. Every close paper used real documents. If the rules were developed against the same 351 fields that show the gain, the figure is a development-set result. By the notes' own calculation, the Wilson 95% interval for 350 of 351 is about 98.4–99.95%. No lab-report OCR paper from Bangladesh was found, and handwriting is the norm for Bangladeshi prescriptions: 97% of doctors write them by hand ([Tabassum et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35246576/)).

## Bangladesh offers burden data and messaging trials, not dish-level plans

The burden case is strong, and every figure in it is citable. In 2024 an estimated **13.9 million Bangladeshi adults had diabetes** (age-standardised 13.2%, 39.1% undiagnosed) ([IDF Diabetes Atlas, 11th ed.](https://international-diabetes-federation.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/media/uploads/sites/3/2025/08/IDF_Atlas_11_2025_SEA_Factsheet.pdf)). The 2022 BDHS measured diabetes in 17% of women and 15% of men, and hypertension in 23% and 17%. Only 5–6% of people with diabetes and 19–20% of people with hypertension were aware, treated and controlled ([NIPORT & ICF 2024](https://www.dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/FR386/FR386.pdf)). In STEPS 2018, adults ate a mean **9.0 g of salt a day**, and 89.6% ate fewer than five servings of fruit and vegetables ([WHO/NIPSOM 2018](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/searo/bangladesh/publications/2018-steps-fact-sheet-bangladesh.pdf)). Anaemia affected an estimated 37.6% of women of reproductive age and 38.6% of pregnant women in 2023 ([WHO GHO](https://ghoapi.azureedge.net/api/NUTRITION_ANAEMIA_REPRODUCTIVEAGE_PREV?$filter=SpatialDim%20eq%20%27BGD%27) [modelled estimates with wide uncertainty]). Two cautions belong with these numbers. The national obesity figures use standard cut-offs, not the WHO Asian cut-offs the engine uses, so they understate overweight as the system defines it. The CKD figure is a pooled 22.48% from heterogeneous local studies ([Banik & Ghosh 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11255-020-02597-6) [95% CI not shown in the record]). The health system cannot provide individual counselling at this scale. Bangladesh has about **7 doctors per 10,000 people** ([WHO GHO, 2023](https://ghoapi.azureedge.net/api/HWF_0001?$filter=SpatialDim%20eq%20%27BGD%27%20and%20TimeDim%20ge%202015)), and its average primary-care consultation of **48 seconds** was the shortest of 67 countries ([Irving et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-017902)). That justifies a pre-built plan that a physician approves. It also means the time a physician takes to review a plan has to be measured before anyone claims the workflow is feasible.

Bangladeshi digital-health work is dominated by one-way messaging, and its record warns against presenting the project as "another app". In the DMagic cluster trial, voice messaging had no effect on diabetes and intermediate hyperglycaemia (aOR 0.93, 0.74–1.16), while community groups did (aOR 0.36) ([Fottrell et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(19)30001-4) [abstract]). For 420 rural adults with hypertension, SMS added nothing over education alone ([Jahan et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.2196/19137)). The clearer gains came where a person was in the loop. Personalised advice papers plus phone messages lowered systolic BP by 7.3 against 4.7 mmHg ([Kobashi et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-023-00574-0)). Digitally supported village clinics cut uncontrolled BP from 60% to 26% ([Xie et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2023-073743) [uncontrolled cohort]). A non-digital programme of community health workers and physician training across Bangladesh, Pakistan and Sri Lanka lowered systolic BP by 5.2 mmHg more than control ([Jafar et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1911965)). The regional recommenders are prototypes. U-HEALTH plans daily meals from Sri Lankan foods but reports no evaluation ([Wijekoon & Harshanath 2023](https://sljoas.uwu.ac.lk/index.php/sljoas/article/view/51)). An Indian English/Hindi dietary decision-support trial randomised 800 people but had no outcomes at publication ([Kumar et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.2196/13635) [protocol]). The Bangladeshi entries are narrow: CKD foods matched to potassium level ([Banerjee et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICCCI.2019.8821871) [abstract]), a fuzzy income-based food list ([Hussain et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICIS.2018.8466403)), and an ML model of nutrient needs with no reported numbers ([OBESEYE, Roy et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02796) [preprint]). Local data resources exist: FoodBD has 3,523 meal images with expert nutrient labels for 1,837 of them ([Ahmed et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-025-07583-8)), alongside FoodNetBD. Clinician-in-the-loop decision support also exists: the Simple app in Bangladesh, and mWellcare in India, which was null on BP and HbA1c ([Prabhakaran et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.118.038192)). A reviewer can fairly say that phone-delivered advice, physician involvement and local food datasets are each already covered. The paper's answer is that none of them produces dish-level plans for several conditions at once from local composition data, behind a physician gate.

Access and literacy limit who a patient-facing web app can reach, and this belongs in the paper's limitations. In Bangladesh, **25% of women own a smartphone against 40% of men**, and 26% of women use mobile internet against 42% of men ([GSMA 2025](https://www.gsma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/The-Mobile-Gender-Gap-Report-2025.pdf)). 43% of 2,793 people using NCD services had limited health literacy ([Salwa et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3928/24748307-20240119-01)). Of the usability problems found in 36 Bangladeshi mHealth apps, 61% were catastrophic or major ([Islam MN et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-020-1033-3)). The groups with the highest anaemia, hypertension and diabetes burden, namely rural women, pregnant women and older adults, are the ones these figures leave out. A clinic-mediated workflow and the printable report partly offset this, and a Bangla interface is necessary but not sufficient. Affordability is a second gap. About 43% of households could not afford the cost of a recommended diet ([Islam S et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods12040790)), and the frequency weighting favours commonly cooked dishes without modelling cost.

## The paper should claim the combination, concede the evidence and fix two flaws

### Related work in four strands and a regional paragraph

The related-work section can follow the notes' structure, with anchor citations for each strand. Diet recommenders anchor on the two recent systematic reviews ([Dong 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/); [Yera 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054248)) and the health-recommender review ([De Croon 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/18035)), and then contrast DIETOS, PROTEIN, Yera Toledo and Papastratis as the closest systems. Clinical decision support and LLMs anchor on Hager 2024 for the general case, Ponzo 2024 or Wang LC 2024 for diet, NutriOrion [preprint] as the soft-constraint contrast, MAI-DIET as the nearest rules-compute, LLM-assists design, and Chen 2024 and Zaretsky 2024 for review of LLM output. Exercise anchors on EXPERT, P3-EX and FITTER [preprint]. OCR anchors on Ma 2023, Neveditsin 2026 and RJUA-MedDQA. A regional paragraph covers the burden figures, the messaging trials and the local datasets (FoodNetBD [dataset], FoodBD, INDB). The notes' positioning sentence works as the section's thesis: earlier diet systems either constrain ML with expert rules without clinician sign-off, or put clinicians over LLM-generated content without deterministic clinical logic, and this system joins the two. The notes also give venue advice. A safety-gated, physician-approved pipeline with synthetic evaluation fits health-informatics venues (JMIR Medical Informatics or Formative Research, BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making, JBI, IJMI, CMPB, PLOS Digital Health, Digital Health) or the HealthRecSys workshop. RecSys main track expects stronger empirical results, and JAMIA-level journals will probably expect the clinician study. The venue, citation style and author list are still to be settled with the author, as `CLAUDE.md` records.

### A novelty claim built element by element

The claim should list each element with its nearest precedent and be worded as a search result, giving the search scope. The notes searched Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, PubMed, arXiv and the web. They could not search IEEE Xplore or DBLP directly, and they were not systematic.

### Limitations the literature makes unavoidable

The limitations in `CLAUDE.md` need to be stated, and the literature turns each one into an expected question. There are no real patients and no clinical evaluation, while the closest peers had clinician or patient contact. The model labels are synthetic, and reviewers will call the evaluation circular. Using synthetic data "as if it is real" leads to models that "do not generalize well to real data" ([van Breugel et al., ICML 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/van-breugel23a.html)). A generator of synthetic patients reproduced demographics but no patient had controlled blood pressure, against 74.5% in real data ([Chen J et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30871520/)). Offline accuracy rankings of recommenders can contradict online results ([Rossetti et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2959100.2959176)). The OCR was tested on synthetic slips only, with possible tuning to the generator. There are no saturated-fat or cholesterol values. The exercise injury ratings are carried but unused, and the exercises have no Bangla names. The Bangla wording has not been reviewed by a clinician. Physician approval is a workflow gate, and the automation-bias evidence above explains why. The literature adds further limitations: no evidence on pre-participation screening; affordability and the digital divide; unmeasured physician review time in a 48-second-consultation system; household frequency standing in for individual preference; no LP or IP baseline for the weekly plan; the optional Claude OCR path, which undercuts the on-premise privacy argument that Neveditsin, MedRepBench [preprint] and Wiest et al. make and needs a stated consent policy; and foreign regulatory frameworks used only as benchmarks. Two of the three code findings also belong here unless they are fixed first: the post-approval phrasing and the tuning leakage.

### Evaluation reviewers will expect

The cheapest step is to tighten the offline study, and the second and third code findings decide how. **Second finding:** [`scripts/train_recommenders.py` line 843](../../scripts/train_recommenders.py#L843) tunes hyperparameters with `GroupKFold` over all archetypes, and [line 861](../../scripts/train_recommenders.py#L861) then evaluates with `GroupKFold` over the same archetypes. The "held-out" archetypes were therefore seen during tuning. When a classifier has been optimised, its cross-validation error is "a substantially biased estimate of the true error", and nested cross-validation is the fix ([Varma & Simon 2006](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2105-7-91)). The bias here is probably small, because the tuned figures sit near the ceiling. Even so, the reported 0.998 (food and recipe) and 0.984 (exercise) are not clean held-out numbers until the outer folds are kept out of the search, and Dong et al. found that 4 of 5 offline diet-recommender evaluations reused training data for testing ([Dong 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809011/)). Reviewers will look for exactly this. The grouped split is still correct in principle, since record-wise validation "often massively overestimates" accuracy ([Saeb et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28327985/)). But holding out single archetypes from a full factorial tests interpolation. Stricter blocks are needed for a claim about extrapolation, such as leaving out one condition, one intake group or a set of items ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881)). The baselines are confounded. scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingRegressor` has no `monotonic_cst`, and `LinearRegression` can only force every coefficient to be positive ([scikit-learn docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.ensemble.GradientBoostingRegressor.html)). A tuned but unconstrained `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` is the missing ablation, given how often tuned simple baselines match complex models ([Ferrari Dacrema et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902)). Patients see only the top of each list, so nDCG@10 and rank-biased overlap should be added to Spearman and top-10 overlap ([Järvelin & Kekäläinen 2002](https://doi.org/10.1145/582415.582418); [Webber et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1145/1852102.1852106)). Per-archetype distributions and confidence intervals by subgroup are also needed (TRIPOD+AI items 12d and 23a, [Collins et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11019967/)), along with a table of every constrained feature, its sign and the guideline behind it. The whole study should be framed as a simulation study in ADEMP form ([Morris et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30652356/)), in which the models are metamodels of a known function ([Degeling et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32428428/)). That framing makes the near-ceiling figures uncontroversial, because it claims nothing beyond fidelity.

**Third finding:** the live app ranks with the shipped trained model when its file exists, and otherwise with the deterministic formula. The hand-written label function g is used only to make the training labels. This has three consequences for the paper. First, it must answer "why ML, if g exists?". The defensible answer in the methods notes is that the trained ranker is the component built to take real physician-review labels later while keeping monotone guarantees, with g serving as a knowledge-based prior. If that is not the plan, a reviewer can fairly ask the authors to ship g. Second, it must report where the shipped model departs from g, including the worst archetypes, because the model, not g, is what patients receive. Third, the two runtime paths rank quite differently: the fallback formula agrees with g at only about 0.79 (food 0.787, recipe 0.794, exercise 0.768, per the [README](../../README.md)). Any result from a running app should therefore name the backend that produced it, which each assessment records in `scoring_backend`.

Beyond the offline study, the notes agree on a staged path, and the next step is the one that matters most. Plan-level metrics against external targets come first and need no participants: plan sodium against 2,000 mg, energy against target, variety across the week, and gate recall on an independently hand-labelled contraindication set. These are the kinds of metrics PROTEIN reported (meal-appropriateness precision 1.000, macronutrient accuracy 92.65%), though the numbers cannot be compared because the data differ ([Stefanidis 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9612332/)). Next comes a **blinded expert-agreement study**. Bangladeshi physicians and dietitians would rate or rank top-10 food, recipe and exercise lists for a stratified sample of vignettes, drawn blind from the model, g, the formula and a dietitian-made list, as in Kim et al.'s design ([Kim 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38577160/)). Expert-expert agreement would be measured with an ICC, and the exercise lists would be coded with Zaleski's ten FITT-VP categories, with two raters per item. FITTER used one reviewer per prescription and reported no agreement statistic. The most informative single number from that study is how well g agrees with the experts, because g is what the models learned. A silent or shadow-mode study follows. Physicians would draft their own selection before seeing the model's, and edits would be logged and turned into pairwise preferences rather than absolute labels. This is reported under DECIDE-AI item 12 on human-AI agreement ([Kwong et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36052317/); [Vasey et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9116198/); [Joachims et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1145/1076034.1076063)). It should measure edit and reject rates, review time, and the catch rate for seeded errors (as in Dratsch's design). A feasibility pilot would then score usability against the SUS benchmark of 68 ([Hyzy et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35980732/)). For the OCR, the single most valuable addition is 50–100 consented real photos of Bangladeshi lab slips. Coverage, accuracy on filled fields and the wrong-value rate should be reported separately, and the engines, a CRF, a compact VLM and the optional Claude path should be run on the same fields, counting false fills. For the report, a check that every approved item and number survives phrasing is needed, or the phrasing fix described above.

## Conclusion

The search changes what the paper is about. Gate-then-rank is established, physician review of AI output is well studied, and Bangladesh has seen many digital-health trials, so none of these can carry a novelty claim alone. What the notes did not find anywhere is a deterministic, cited, test-enforced pipeline from Bangladeshi dish data to a physician-approved plan. That is a modest claim, and an honest one if it is worded as a search result. The physician gate is at once the project's most distinctive feature and its least evidenced. Its value depends on whether review is meaningful, which the automation-bias literature says cannot be assumed, and on whether the patient reads what the physician approved, which the current report route does not ensure. Moving LLM phrasing in front of approval turns the project's weakest claim into one of its cleanest, at little engineering cost.

The second shift concerns what must be validated next. The model's fidelity to the label function is already near its ceiling, and the paper should report it as a simulation result, with nested cross-validation, and stop there. The object the next study must test is the label function itself, against Bangladeshi physicians and dietitians, with the model, the function and the formula compared side by side. If the function holds up, the trained ranker has a clear role as the part that learns from physicians' edits. If it does not, the disagreements are the data the project currently lacks.
